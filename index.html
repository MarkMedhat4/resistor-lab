    Resistor color-code theory

    Eight short lessons covering everything you need to read a resistor with confidence.

    01 What is a resistor?

    A resistor is a passive electronic component that limits or controls the flow of electric current in a circuit. It converts excess electrical energy into heat, which lets engineers set exact current and voltage levels for the rest of the circuit to work correctly.

    Resistors are measured in ohms (Ω), and most through-hole resistors are too small to print their value as text — so manufacturers use colored bands instead.

    02 What is resistor color coding?

    Resistor color coding is a standardized system (IEC 60062) that represents a resistor's digits, multiplier, tolerance and sometimes temperature coefficient as a sequence of painted bands around the body.

    Each color stands for a fixed value — for example, brown always means the digit 1 or a ×10 multiplier, depending on which band it occupies. Once you know the sequence of colors and their role in that position, you can calculate the resistance without any text printed on the part.

    03 How to read a 4-band resistor

    A 4-band resistor is the most common type. Its bands mean:

    • Band 1 — first significant digit
    • Band 2 — second significant digit
    • Band 3 — multiplier
    • Band 4 — tolerance

    Example: Brown, Red, Orange, Gold → digits 1 and 2, multiplied by ×1,000, giving 12,000 Ω (12 kΩ), with a tolerance of ±5%.

    04 How to read a 5-band resistor

    5-band resistors add a third significant digit for higher precision:

    • Band 1 — first significant digit
    • Band 2 — second significant digit
    • Band 3 — third significant digit
    • Band 4 — multiplier
    • Band 5 — tolerance

    Example: Brown, Black, Black, Red, Brown → digits 1, 0, 0, multiplied by ×100, giving 10,000 Ω (10 kΩ), with a tolerance of ±1%.

    05 How to read a 6-band resistor

    6-band resistors are identical to 5-band resistors, with one extra band added at the end for the temperature coefficient:

    • Bands 1–5 — same as a 5-band resistor
    • Band 6 — temperature coefficient (ppm/K)

    The temperature coefficient tells you how much the resistance drifts, in parts per million, for every 1°C (1 Kelvin) change in temperature — important for precision circuits exposed to varying temperatures.

    06 What is tolerance?

    Tolerance is the maximum percentage the actual resistance may differ from the printed (nominal) value, due to normal manufacturing variation.

    A 1,000 Ω resistor with ±5% tolerance could measure anywhere between 950 Ω and 1,050 Ω and still be considered within spec. Tighter tolerances (±1%, ±0.5%, ±0.1%) cost more but are needed in precision measurement and analog circuits.

    07 What is temperature coefficient?

    The temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR) describes how much a resistor's value changes as its temperature changes, expressed in parts per million per Kelvin (ppm/K).

    A resistor rated at 50 ppm/K will change its resistance by 50 millionths of its nominal value for every 1 K change in temperature. Lower numbers mean a more temperature-stable resistor — critical for precision references and measurement instruments.

    08 How to identify the reading direction

    A resistor should normally be read from the end where the first significant band begins. The tolerance band is often spaced further apart from the other bands and is commonly gold or silver, which helps mark it as the last band.

    Important: physical resistor markings can vary between manufacturers, and spacing is not an infallible identification method. When in doubt, measure the resistor with a multimeter and compare it against both possible readings — the correct reading is the one that matches a standard resistor value.
